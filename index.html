Orivé

Orivé is a holistic wellness app designed to bring physical and mental wellbeing into one connected experience. It combines fitness, mood, sleep, habits, expert support and AI-powered guidance so young adults can manage their wellbeing without navigating multiple disconnected platforms.

Role

UX Research · Product Design · UI Design

Year

2026

Tools

Figma

Type

Personal project

01 Problem

Wellbeing is spread across too many apps.

Young adults often manage fitness, sleep, mood, habits and mental wellbeing across separate apps, creating a fragmented experience that becomes difficult to maintain when life gets busy or stressful. Orivé explores how these parts of wellbeing can exist within one personal, supportive experience without making health tracking feel like another responsibility.

02 Research

Interviews, a survey and a competitive audit

I wrote a 14-question interview guide and a 15-question survey about routines, stress, support and app habits, then compared how Apple Health, Headspace and Reflectly cover different parts of wellbeing. I synthesised responses in an affinity map and empathy map.

“Too many notifications, gets annoying and I delete it.”
“Tracking everything becomes too much work and also using 4 different apps makes it feel repetitive.”

03 Synthesis
& pain points

The consistency gap

People want to be healthy but struggle to keep routines when workload and stress rise.

Too much effort

Existing apps need too much tracking, send too many notifications and lock features behind paywalls.

Fragmented tools

Fitness, sleep, mood and habits live in different apps, so nothing feels connected.

Careful trust in AI

Most are open to AI guidance, but accuracy, privacy and losing human support are real concerns.

05 Problem definition

How might we bring fitness, mood, sleep, habits and support into one experience that stays easy to keep up with, even in stressful weeks?

→ product decisions

  4. Emotional tracking feels like workQuick, emoji-based mood check-ins.

CrewCares

CrewCares explores how environmental and health information could be made actionable for NYC subway workers and union stakeholders, connecting a worker-facing experience with a union dashboard. A prototype built at the Health in Climate AI hackathon.

Role

Product Design · UX · Product Strategy

Team

Team of 6

Tools

Figma, Figma Make, Claude

Event · Year

Health in Climate AI Hackathon · 2026

Test the product ↗
01 Context

30 hours at Cornell Tech

CrewCares was built at the Health in Climate AI Hackathon during Climate Week NYC, with teammates from technical and research backgrounds and input from mentors across product, clinical and public health.

02 Problem

Subway workers don’t choose where they work.

Assignments come from a seniority-bidding system, so the environmental conditions of a station are never factored in. Workers spend hours in those conditions with little information about them.

03 Research &
environmental evidence

What the underground can mean for health

We looked at the conditions that matter most underground: particulate matter, heat, humidity, flooding and ventilation, and what they could mean for people spending full shifts in them.

04 Data limitations

The data we needed didn’t exist.

There is no public per-station air-quality feed for the NYC subway. Instead, the team worked from available above-ground weather and air-quality readings and explored ways of estimating conditions underground.

05 Opportunity

Give workers information that makes sense for today’s shift, and give their union a clearer picture of where attention may be needed.

06 System architecture

One system. Two sides of the same problem.

Workers need environmental information that makes sense in the context of their shift. Union stakeholders need aggregated information that helps them identify where attention may be needed. CrewCares connects those perspectives without turning the worker experience into a complex dashboard.

Worker

On the phone

  • Understand conditions
  • Receive precautions
  • Report concerns
Shared data
Union

On the dashboard

  • See patterns
  • Identify stations needing attention
  • Turn reports into action
07 Worker experience

Guidance that fits the shift

I focused on turning the team’s research and environmental data into something a worker could act on in seconds: understand today’s conditions, get a precaution, and report a concern.

08 Union dashboard

Where attention may be needed

The union side translates environmental and worker-reported information into station-level insight: a live risk map, stations ranked for attention, and member reports grouped by issue.

Figures from the working demo. Platform conditions are modelled and member reports are simulated.

09 How the data works

Measured, modelled and simulated

Measured / source

Inputs

  • Live outdoor weather and air-quality readings
  • NYC subway station list: structure type and routes served
Modelled / estimated

Platform conditions

  • Platform PM2.5, temperature and humidity
  • Flood and mould risk
  • A 1–5 risk level per station
Simulated

Demo reports

  • Worker complaints in the demo are a synthetic set, not real submissions

No official live MTA underground sensor data was used. Modelled figures show where to look first, not whether a station is safe.

10 Design decisions
  1. Meet workers where they areThe worker side runs in a text thread. No app to install.
  2. Instructions, not evidenceAlerts lead with what to do today, like wearing an N95.
  3. Separate health from reportsHealth answers stay on the phone. A complaint carries only station, date and the worker’s words.
  4. Label every estimateModelled and simulated figures are marked wherever they appear.

12 Reflection

Designing with data

This project changed how I think about designing with data. I entered the weekend most comfortable with UX and interface design, but working with teammates across technical and research backgrounds taught me how design can become the bridge between complex information and the people who need to act on it.

We didn’t get the outcome we hoped for, but I left with a new way of approaching problems and more confidence working in rooms where I’m not the expert.

AI Tarot Reader

A small web experiment: ask a question, choose your mood and draw a card. The reading combines a card pulled from a tarot API with a message written for how you feel.

Role

Built with

01 Concept

A reading that starts with how you feel

Tarot is a ritual of asking. I wanted to see how a simple interface could turn a random draw into a moment that feels personal, by letting the user’s mood shape the tone of the reading.

02 Interaction
Ask a question→Choose a mood→Draw my card→Card + meaning + reading

Five moods: confused, anxious, hopeful, stuck and excited. Everything happens on one screen.

03 How it works
RequestOn click, the script calls the tarot API with fetch.
DrawIt picks one random card from the deck it returns.
MeaningIt shows the card’s upright meaning from the API.
MoodIt adds a short reading I wrote for the selected mood.
04 Visual & interaction system

A frosted-glass card floats on a deep purple gradient, with a classic serif for a slightly mystical, bookish feel. The button lifts and brightens on hover.

const drawBtn = document.getElementById("drawBtn");

const result = document.getElementById("result");

drawBtn.addEventListener("click", drawCard);

async function drawCard(){

    const question =
    document.getElementById("questionInput").value;

    const mood =
    document.getElementById("moodInput").value;

    const response =
    await fetch("https://tarotapi.dev/api/v1/cards");

    const data = await response.json();

    const cards = data.cards;

    const randomCard =
    cards[Math.floor(Math.random() * cards.length)];

    let message = "";

    if(mood === "anxious"){
        message =
        "You may be overthinking the unknown right now. Trust that clarity will come.";
    }

    else if(mood === "hopeful"){
        message =
        "This card reflects growth, softness, and possibility entering your life.";
    }

    else if(mood === "stuck"){
        message =
        "A shift in perspective may help you move forward.";
    }

    else if(mood === "excited"){
        message =
        "Follow your excitement, but remember to stay grounded.";
    }

    else{
        message =
        "Not every answer appears immediately. Sit with uncertainty.";
    }

    result.style.display = "block";

    result.innerHTML = `
        <h2>${randomCard.name}</h2>

        <p>
        <strong>Your Question:</strong>
        ${question}
        </p>

        <p>
        <strong>Card Meaning:</strong>
        ${randomCard.meaning_up}
        </p>

        <p>
        <strong>Your Reading:</strong>
        ${message}
        </p>
    `;
}

07 What I learned

Working with live data

Building the Tarot Reader introduced me to working with live external data and dynamically translating it into an interactive experience. Using JavaScript, fetch and async/await, I learned how information from an API could become part of the interface rather than static content.

The project also made me think about the difference between retrieving information and interpreting it. A future exploration would be using a language model to respond to the question itself, which the current version does not do.

Before It Makes Sense

A fragmented environment that only becomes understandable when you change how you look at it.

Medium

Unity and projection mapping using MadMapper

Discipline

Interaction Design · Experience Design

Year

2026

Role

Solo project

01 Concept

The environment stays the same. Perception changes.

This projection-mapped installation explores the uncertainty of being inside a transition before you can understand what it means. It grew from my experience of changing academic directions, when something I had planned around suddenly stopped making sense. Rather than representing that experience literally, I translated the feeling of uncertainty into an environment that could only be understood gradually.

Built in Unity and projected across suspended fabric and paper, the installation breaks a single environment into disconnected visual fragments. From one position, viewers encounter partial images that feel unrelated and incomplete. As they physically move through the space, those fragments begin to overlap, align, and reveal that they have been looking at different pieces of the same environment all along.

The environment itself never changes. What changes is the viewer’s position, context, and understanding.

Nothing in the environment changes. Only the viewer’s perspective does.

02 Experimentation

Fabric and paper catch the projections unevenly, so each surface shows only part of the image.

03 Fragments → the whole

Stills from the installation film, in order: fragment, then fragment, then more context, until the room comes together.

Installation film, 1:18.

04 Reflection

The project is about the period before something makes sense: when individual moments can feel disconnected or even like failure, but gain meaning once they are seen as part of a larger picture.
