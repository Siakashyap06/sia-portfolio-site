Orivé

Designing one calm place for body and mind, for young adults whose wellness routines fall apart when life gets busy.

Problem

Young adults track fitness, sleep and mood across separate apps, and their routines are the first thing to go when stress rises.

What I did

The full product design process, solo: interviews, a survey and competitive analysis, persona, IA, sketches, wireframes, visual exploration, hi-fi UI and usability testing.

Outcome

A complete hi-fi iOS design of 30+ screens that brings workouts, daily logs, AI guidance and expert support into one app.

Project

Orivé · Personal case study

Year

2026

Timeline

12 weeks

Tools

Figma, FigJam

01 Overview

One app for the whole of wellbeing

Orivé is a holistic wellness app that brings physical and mental wellbeing into one connected experience. It combines fitness, mood, sleep, habits, expert support and AI-powered guidance, so young adults can understand and manage their wellbeing without switching between disconnected platforms.

I designed it end to end on my own, from the first interview question to the final screen, which meant I owned every decision along the way.

02 Impact
  • Turned interviews, a survey and a 15-parameter competitive analysis into four insights that widened Orivé from fitness into stress, sleep, mood and expert support.
  • Restructured the app from four tabs to five, giving AI guidance its own home and moving everyday tracking into a dedicated Logs tab.
  • Usability tested the first hi-fi designs and made six key changes, from a dedicated My AI tab to a complete booking flow.
  • Designed 30+ high-fidelity screens across onboarding, an 8-question health assessment, workouts, logs, My AI and a full booking flow.
03 The challenge

Support without another chore

Young adults often manage fitness, sleep, mood, habits and mental wellbeing across multiple apps. That fragmented experience becomes hard to keep up when life gets busy or stressful.

The hard part wasn’t fitting more health features into one app. It was deciding what deserved attention and what could stay out of the way, so tracking wellbeing never felt like another responsibility.

Across interviews, survey responses and competitive research, one tension kept coming up: people wanted support and visibility into their wellbeing, but not another system that demanded constant attention.

People want to maintain healthy routines, but consistency drops when workload and stress increase.

08 The design question

How might we bring fitness, mood, sleep, habits and support into one experience that stays easy to maintain, even during stressful weeks?

09 Key decisions

Research shaped four key decisions

01

One question at a time

Research

Long questionnaires felt overwhelming and caused drop-off.

Decision

An 8-question health assessment that shows one question per screen, with a progress indicator and a skip option on every step.

02

Earning trust with health data

Research

People hesitate to share health data, and trust in AI depends on accuracy and privacy.

Decision

Explain why data is needed during setup and give users control over it, with AI guidance kept separate from human support.

03

Start small

Research

Too many habits at once create pressure and reduce motivation.

Decision

Start with one or two manageable habits, shown as a short to-do list on Home next to the workout streak.

04

Check-ins in seconds

Research

Mood tracking can feel like work.

Decision

Quick visual check-ins: tap how you feel and pick an emotion, instead of writing long entries.

14 Usability testing
& iterations

What testing changed

I put my first high-fidelity designs in front of users and asked them to complete core tasks: get set up, find a workout, ask the AI a question and book an expert. Watching where they hesitated led to six key changes.

01

AI gets its own tab

AI lived inside Categories as an “AI mode” toggle, mixed in with browsing. It is now My AI, a dedicated tab that opens with suggested prompts.

Why · from testing In testing, users missed the small “AI mode” button and weren’t sure whether they were talking to AI or an expert. A dedicated tab made AI easy to find and kept it clearly separate from human support.

02

Four tabs became five

Home, Category, Track and Profile became Home, Categories, My AI, Logs and Profile.

Why · from testing Testers looked for a place to log their day and couldn’t find one under “Track”. A Logs tab and a My AI tab matched what they were looking for.

03

A calmer Home

Six cards (wellness score, steps and miles, goal overview, to-dos and a promo) became three focused blocks: workout streak, today’s activity and a short to-do list.

Why · from testing Testers found the first Home crowded and weren’t sure what to look at first. Three focused blocks made the daily overview quick to scan.

04

Clearer categories

Pill filters, a sort menu and a “See all” link became three icon tabs with an underline, and each tab shows all twelve of its categories at once.

Why · from testing Testers didn’t notice the filters and sort menu. Clear icon tabs made it obvious which area they were in and how to switch.

05

From “Connect” to booking

A profile with a single Connect button became a profile with available dates, leading to a time picker and a booking confirmation.

Why · from testing When users tapped “Connect”, they weren’t sure what would happen next. Showing dates and times turned it into a booking flow they could finish.

06

A warmer visual system

Green with serif accents became deep maroon on warm cream, set entirely in IBM Plex Sans.

Why · from testing Testers described the green design as clinical. In side-by-side comparisons, users preferred maroon and IBM Plex Sans as warmer and more personal.

15 Final design

One home for your everyday wellness.

A daily overview brings progress, routines and priorities together without asking users to check multiple parts of the app.

Personalized onboarding

Start with what matters to you.

A short assessment captures goals, activity, stress, sleep, water, obstacles and motivation, one question at a time, creating a relevant starting point without an overwhelming setup.

Everyday wellness

Physical and mental wellbeing, in one place.

Workouts, mental health resources and everyday tracking live in the same experience, so moving between parts of wellbeing takes one tap.

AI + human support

Guidance when you need it, people when you need more.

My AI offers lightweight everyday guidance, while professional support stays a separate, visible path for when users need more than a recommendation.

CrewCares

Giving NYC subway workers a say in their own health and safety, and giving their union the data to act on it.

Problem

Subway workers are assigned stations by seniority, so heat, dust and poor ventilation are never factored in, and workers have little information about the conditions they spend full shifts in.

What I did

Product strategy, UX and interface design on a 5-person team: from greybox wireframes to the worker text flows and the union operations dashboard.

Outcome

A working prototype in 30 hours: an SMS service for workers and a live dashboard for the union, built on real outdoor data and clearly labelled estimates.

Event

Cornell Tech × Health in Climate AI Hackathon

Role

UX & Product Lead

Year

2026

Tools

Figma, Figma Make, Claude Code

01 Overview

Designing for health below the city

CrewCares was built at the Health in Climate AI Hackathon at Cornell Tech during Climate Week NYC. It is a text-message service that tells transit workers what conditions to expect at their station and lets them report hazards in one sentence, paired with a dashboard that shows the union where attention is needed.

I worked alongside teammates from technical and research backgrounds, with input from mentors across product, clinical and public health. My focus was turning the team’s research and environmental data into an experience both workers and union stakeholders could understand.

02 Impact
  • Took the product from a standalone app to a simple message extension, so workers never have to learn a new system, download anything or log in.
  • Designed a two-sided system: private, shift-specific guidance for workers and a station-level operations dashboard for the union.
  • Iterated the dashboard from a greybox wireframe to a report-count view to a final risk-first operations dashboard across 496 stations.
  • Made uncertainty visible: every modelled or simulated figure is labelled, so the dashboard shows where to look, not whether a station is safe.
03 The challenge

Subway workers don’t choose where they work

Assignments come from a seniority-bidding system, so the environmental conditions of a station are never factored in. Workers spend hours underground with little information about the air they breathe or the heat they work in.

The hard part wasn’t showing more data. It was deciding what each person actually needed to know, what had to stay private, and how to be honest about numbers that were estimates.

04 Research

What the underground can mean for health

We focused on the conditions that matter most underground and what prolonged exposure could mean for people spending full shifts in them.

Particulate matter (PM2.5)HeatHumidityFloodingVentilationMould
The data gap

There is no public, live station-level air-quality feed for the NYC subway. We had to design around missing data instead of pretending it existed.

05 Who it’s for

Two users, two very different needs

Transit worker

On the phone, before a shift

  • Know today’s conditions at their station
  • Get one clear precaution
  • Report a hazard without paperwork
  • Keep personal health data private
Shared data
Union rep

On the dashboard, across the system

  • See patterns across stations
  • Spot where reports and risk overlap
  • Prioritize which stations to raise
  • Trust what the numbers mean
06 The design question

How might we give workers information they can act on today, while giving their union a clear picture of where attention is needed, without overstating what the data can tell us?

07 Key decisions

Four decisions that shaped the system

01

A message extension, not a new app

Insight

Transit workers span a wide range of ages and comfort with technology, and many could find a whole new system hard to navigate, especially on shift and underground.

Decision

We built CrewCares as a simple extension of the messages app workers already use: a text 90 minutes before the shift, and replies in plain words. Nothing new to download, learn or log into.

02

Lead with action, not data

Insight

A PM2.5 reading means little at 6am before a shift.

Decision

Alerts lead with what to do today, like wearing an N95, with the reading as supporting detail.

03

Separate health from reports

Insight

Workers won’t speak up if it could expose something medical.

Decision

Health check-ins stay on the worker’s phone. A complaint carries only the station, the date and the worker’s own words.

04

Label every estimate

Insight

Underground conditions had to be modelled from above-ground data.

Decision

Every modelled or simulated figure is marked where it appears, so the dashboard points to where to look rather than declaring a station safe or unsafe.

08 Early concept

From a new app to a message extension

Our first idea was a standalone app with an employee login. But many workers could find a whole new system hard to navigate, so we moved the worker experience into a simple message extension inside the messages app they already use.

Nothing new to learn

No download, no login and no new navigation. Workers get guidance and report hazards in a thread that works like any other text conversation.

09 Dashboard iterations

From greybox to operations dashboard

The union dashboard went through three rounds in 30 hours.

Round 1 · Greybox wireframe

I blocked out the layout with counts of reports, stations and recurring cases, a subway map, a trend chart and reports by concern. My notes list what was still missing: AI insights, an interactive map, worker comments, top 5 stations and map filters.

Round 2 · Report-first dashboard

The first hi-fi version added the missing pieces, but it was built around counting complaints. A station with no reports looked fine, even if its conditions were poor.

Round 3 · Final operations dashboard

The final dashboard leads with modelled risk across all 496 stations and highlights where risk and worker reports overlap. Selecting a station shows its readings and recent reports, and AI-assisted recommendations sit in their own labelled panel.

Reports → risk + reportsStations with no complaints are no longer invisible.
A station detail panelReadings and worker quotes for one station, side by side.
Clear labels“Modelled” and “AI-assisted” wherever estimates appear.

async function drawCard(){

    const question =
    document.getElementById("questionInput").value;

    const mood =
    document.getElementById("moodInput").value;

    const response =
    await fetch("https://tarotapi.dev/api/v1/cards");

    const data = await response.json();

    const cards = data.cards;

    const randomCard =
    cards[Math.floor(Math.random() * cards.length)];

    let message = "";

    if(mood === "anxious"){
        message =
        "You may be overthinking the unknown right now. Trust that clarity will come.";
    }

    else if(mood === "hopeful"){
        message =
        "This card reflects growth, softness, and possibility entering your life.";
    }

    else if(mood === "stuck"){
        message =
        "A shift in perspective may help you move forward.";
    }

    else if(mood === "excited"){
        message =
        "Follow your excitement, but remember to stay grounded.";
    }

    else{
        message =
        "Not every answer appears immediately. Sit with uncertainty.";
    }

    result.style.display = "block";

    result.innerHTML = `
        <h2>${randomCard.name}</h2>

        <p>
        <strong>Your Question:</strong>
        ${question}
        </p>

        <p>
        <strong>Card Meaning:</strong>
        ${randomCard.meaning_up}
        </p>

        <p>
        <strong>Your Reading:</strong>
        ${message}
        </p>
    `;
}

Before It Makes Sense

A fragmented environment that only becomes understandable when you change how you look at it.

Medium

Unity and projection mapping using MadMapper

Discipline

Interaction Design · Experience Design

Year

2026

Role

Solo project

01 Concept

The environment stays the same. Perception changes.

This projection-mapped installation explores the uncertainty of being inside a transition before you can understand what it means. It grew from my experience of changing academic directions, when something I had planned around suddenly stopped making sense. Rather than representing that experience literally, I translated the feeling of uncertainty into an environment that could only be understood gradually.

Built in Unity and projected across suspended fabric and paper, the installation breaks a single environment into disconnected visual fragments. From one position, viewers encounter partial images that feel unrelated and incomplete. As they physically move through the space, those fragments begin to overlap, align, and reveal that they have been looking at different pieces of the same environment all along.

The environment itself never changes. What changes is the viewer’s position, context, and understanding.

Nothing in the environment changes. Only the viewer’s perspective does.

02 Experimentation

Fabric and paper catch the projections unevenly, so each surface shows only part of the image.

03 Fragments → the whole

Stills from the installation film, in order: fragment, then fragment, then more context, until the room comes together.

Installation film, 1:18.

04 Reflection

The project is about the period before something makes sense: when individual moments can feel disconnected or even like failure, but gain meaning once they are seen as part of a larger picture.
